\section{From steepest descent to stochastic approximation}

Iterative descent has roots in numerical analysis rather than in modern neural
network terminology. In 1847 Augustin-Louis Cauchy described a method of moving
along steepest descent while solving systems arising from quadratic
problems.\footnote{A.-L. Cauchy, ``M\'ethode g\'en\'erale pour la r\'esolution
des syst\`emes d'\'equations simultan\'ees,'' \emph{Comptes rendus de
l'Acad\'emie des Sciences}, 25, 1847.} In 1951 Herbert Robbins and Sutton Monro
gave a stochastic approximation procedure for finding a solution when only
noisy experimental responses were available.\footnote{H. Robbins and S.
Monro, ``A Stochastic Approximation Method,'' \emph{The Annals of Mathematical
Statistics}, 22(3), 1951,
\url{https://doi.org/10.1214/aoms/1177729586}.}

Acceleration also predates the current deep-learning vocabulary. Boris Polyak
analyzed the heavy-ball method in 1964, adding a fraction of the preceding
displacement to the next step.
\footnote{B. T. Polyak, ``Some methods of speeding up the convergence of
iteration methods,'' \emph{USSR Computational Mathematics and Mathematical
Physics}, 4(5), 1964,
\url{https://doi.org/10.1016/0041-5553(64)90137-5}.}
Yurii Nesterov's 1983 method obtained an accelerated worst-case rate for smooth
convex optimization through a carefully constructed look-ahead sequence.
\footnote{Y. E. Nesterov, ``A method for solving the convex programming
problem with convergence rate $O(1/k^2)$,'' \emph{Doklady Akademii Nauk SSSR},
269(3), 1983, \url{https://www.mathnet.ru/eng/dan46009}.}
These methods are still first order: they use objective values and gradients,
not the Hessian matrix required by a second-order method such as Newton's
method.

The neuron language has a separate history. McCulloch and Pitts formalized
networks of thresholded logical units in 1943, and Rosenblatt developed the
trainable perceptron in the 1950s.
\footnote{W. S. McCulloch and W. Pitts, ``A logical calculus of the ideas
immanent in nervous activity,'' \emph{Bulletin of Mathematical Biophysics}, 5,
1943, \url{https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02478259}; F. Rosenblatt, ``The perceptron:
a probabilistic model for information storage and organization in the brain,''
\emph{Psychological Review}, 65(6), 1958,
\url{https://doi.org/10.1037/h0042519}.}
Those units used threshold behavior. The identity-activation unit studied here
is deliberately different: it supplies the smallest computational graph that
is exactly affine regression and makes the later transition to differentiable
networks mathematically transparent.

Modern gradient-based learning joins these two ideas: use local derivative
information to improve an objective, and estimate that information from a
manageable sample when the complete sum is expensive. Automatic
differentiation changes how derivatives are computed, not what optimization
question they answer.

\section{One variable: the derivative proposes a local move}

For differentiable $J:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}$, Taylor's formula gives
\[
J(w+\Delta w)=J(w)+J'(w)\Delta w+O((\Delta w)^2).
\]
When $J'(w)>0$, a small negative $\Delta w$ lowers the linear approximation;
when $J'(w)<0$, a small positive move does. Choosing
\[
\Delta w=-\eta J'(w),\qquad \eta>0,
\]
produces the gradient-descent iteration
\[
w_{t+1}=w_t-\eta_tJ'(w_t).
\]

The derivative supplies local direction and sensitivity, not a complete route
to a global minimizer. The learning rate $\eta_t$ converts sensitivity into a
step. If the step is too large, the neglected curvature term can dominate and
the objective may increase.

\begin{theorem}[Exact convergence on a scalar quadratic]
\label{thm:scalar-gd}
Let $J(w)=\tfrac12a(w-w^*)^2$ with $a>0$, and apply gradient descent with a
constant learning rate $\eta$. Then
\[
w_t-w^*=(1-\eta a)^t(w_0-w^*).
\]
For every starting point, the iterates converge to $w^*$ if and only if
$0<\eta<2/a$. The choice $\eta=1/a$ reaches $w^*$ in one update.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Because $J'(w)=a(w-w^*)$, subtracting $w^*$ from the update gives
$w_{t+1}-w^*=(1-\eta a)(w_t-w^*)$. Repeated substitution proves the formula.
The geometric sequence converges to zero exactly when
$|1-\eta a|<1$, which is equivalent to $0<\eta<2/a$. At $\eta=1/a$ the
contraction factor is zero.
\end{proof}

The sign and magnitude of $1-\eta a$ explain the whole trajectory. A positive
factor approaches from one side, a negative factor oscillates, magnitude above
one diverges, and a factor close to one makes slow progress. This exact example
is the first model for diagnosing a learning curve rather than merely watching
it.

\conceptcheck{For $J(w)=5(w-3)^2/2$, classify the behavior expected at
$\eta=0.05$, $0.2$, $0.35$, and $0.45$. Which choice reaches the minimizer in
one exact step?}

\section{Several variables: the gradient represents all directions}

For $J:\mathbb{R}^p\rightarrow\mathbb{R}$, the local approximation is
\[
J(\theta+d)\approx J(\theta)+\nabla J(\theta)^Td.
\]
For a unit direction $u$, the directional derivative is
\[
D_uJ(\theta)=\nabla J(\theta)^Tu.
\]
\begin{theorem}[The negative gradient is Euclidean steepest descent]
\label{thm:steepest-descent}
If $\nabla J(\theta)\ne0$, then among all unit Euclidean directions,
\[
\min_{\lVert u\rVert_2=1}D_uJ(\theta)
=-\lVert\nabla J(\theta)\rVert_2,
\]
and the unique minimizing direction is
$u=-\nabla J(\theta)/\lVert\nabla J(\theta)\rVert_2$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Cauchy-Schwarz gives
$\nabla J(\theta)^Tu\ge
-\lVert\nabla J(\theta)\rVert_2\lVert u\rVert_2
=-\lVert\nabla J(\theta)\rVert_2$.
Equality occurs when $u$ is a negative scalar multiple of the gradient; the
unit-length constraint fixes the stated direction.
\end{proof}

The phrase ``steepest'' depends on geometry. Rescaling parameters changes
Euclidean distance and therefore changes the gradient path. A preconditioner,
natural gradient, or adaptive method corresponds to a different geometry or
coordinate-dependent scaling. This is why units and representation affect
optimization even when they leave the set of fitted predictions unchanged.

The Hessian
\[
H(\theta)=\nabla^2J(\theta)
\]
describes local curvature. Positive semidefinite $H$ throughout a convex domain
implies a convex objective. At a differentiable convex minimum the gradient is
zero, but a zero gradient in a nonconvex problem may also identify a saddle or
maximum.

\begin{misconceptionbox}
\textbf{Misconception:} the gradient points toward the nearest global minimum.
\textbf{Correction:} it points in the direction of steepest infinitesimal
decrease under a chosen norm. Curvature, constraints, step size, and nonconvex
geometry determine what happens after a finite step.
\end{misconceptionbox}

\section{Quadratic objectives make curvature visible}

Consider
\[
J(\theta)=\frac12\theta^TH\theta-c^T\theta+d,
\qquad H=H^T\succ 0.
\]
Then $\nabla J=H\theta-c$ and the unique minimizer is
$\theta^*=H^{-1}c$. Gradient descent satisfies
\[
\theta_{t+1}-\theta^*=(I-\eta H)(\theta_t-\theta^*).
\]
If $0<\lambda_{\min}\leq\lambda_j(H)\leq\lambda_{\max}$, a constant step
converges when
\[
0<\eta<\frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}}.
\]

The condition number $\kappa=\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}$ measures the
curvature disparity. A large $\kappa$ produces elongated contours. A safe step
for the steep direction makes little progress along the flat direction, so the
path zigzags slowly. Centering, scaling, reparameterization, and
preconditioning attempt to improve this geometry.

\begin{theorem}[Descent lemma and a safe fixed step]
\label{thm:descent-lemma}
Suppose $J$ has an $L$-Lipschitz gradient:
$\lVert\nabla J(v)-\nabla J(u)\rVert_2\le L\lVert v-u\rVert_2$. Then
\[
J(v)\leq J(u)+\nabla J(u)^T(v-u)+\frac{L}{2}\lVert v-u\rVert_2^2.
\]
Consequently, with $v=u-\eta\nabla J(u)$,
\[
J(v)\le J(u)-\eta\left(1-\frac{L\eta}{2}\right)
\lVert\nabla J(u)\rVert_2^2.
\]
Thus $0<\eta<2/L$ strictly decreases the objective whenever the gradient is
nonzero.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $d=v-u$ and $h(t)=J(u+td)$. The fundamental theorem of calculus gives
\[
J(v)-J(u)=\int_0^1\nabla J(u+td)^Td\,dt.
\]
Add and subtract $\nabla J(u)^Td$. Cauchy-Schwarz and Lipschitz continuity
bound the remaining integrand by $Lt\lVert d\rVert_2^2$. Integration from zero
to one gives the first inequality. Substituting
$d=-\eta\nabla J(u)$ and collecting terms gives the second.
\end{proof}

\section{Least squares supplies an exact laboratory}

For design matrix $X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}$ and target $y$, define
\[
J(w)=\frac{1}{2n}\lVert Xw-y\rVert_2^2.
\]
Differentiation gives
\[
\nabla J(w)=\frac1nX^T(Xw-y),
\qquad
\nabla^2J(w)=\frac1nX^TX.
\]
The Hessian is positive semidefinite, so every local minimum is global. If $X$
has full column rank, the minimum is unique. If not, gradient descent still
minimizes fitted loss but the coefficient selected depends on initialization
and the geometry of the updates.

The largest eigenvalue of $X^TX/n$ controls the stable constant step. Feature
scaling changes that spectrum. This gives a precise reason to scale before
gradient descent rather than the vague rule that models always require
standardization.

\begin{theorem}[An identity neuron is affine linear regression]
\label{thm:identity-neuron}
Let a single computational unit have weights $w\in\mathbb{R}^p$, bias
$b\in\mathbb{R}$, preactivation $z=w^Tx+b$, and identity activation
$\phi(z)=z$. Its hypothesis class is exactly the class of affine linear
regression functions. If it is trained by mean squared error, its objective and
parameter gradients are exactly those of ordinary least squares with an
intercept.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The forward map is
$\widehat y=\phi(w^Tx+b)=w^Tx+b$, which is the affine regression map. Conversely,
every affine regression function is represented by the same $w$ and $b$ in the
unit. For
\[
J(w,b)=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^n(w^Tx_i+b-y_i)^2,
\]
the chain rule through $z_i$ and $\phi'(z_i)=1$ gives
\[
\nabla_wJ=\frac1n\sum_i(\widehat y_i-y_i)x_i,
\qquad
\frac{\partial J}{\partial b}=\frac1n\sum_i(\widehat y_i-y_i),
\]
the ordinary least-squares gradients. Representation equivalence does not
force training equivalence: a direct QR solve and gradient descent can produce
the same fitted function by different numerical routes.
\end{proof}

\begin{workedexample}{a linear neuron on real clinical measurements}
Let $x_i$ be a standardized baseline BMI measurement and let $y_i$ be a
one-year disease-progression score from the diabetes teaching table. A single
unit with identity activation computes
\[
z_i=wx_i+b,\qquad \widehat y_i=a(z_i)=z_i.
\]
With
\[
J(w,b)=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^n( wx_i+b-y_i)^2,
\]
the chain rule gives
\[
\frac{\partial J}{\partial w}
=\frac1n\sum_i(\widehat y_i-y_i)x_i,
\qquad
\frac{\partial J}{\partial b}
=\frac1n\sum_i(\widehat y_i-y_i).
\]
These are exactly the least-squares gradients. ``Single neuron'' and ``linear
regression'' describe two views of the same function and objective here. The
neuron language becomes informative when activation, composition, or shared
optimization later changes; it does not create a new estimator by itself.

Plot three synchronized objects: contours of $J(w,b)$ in parameter space, the
line $wx+b$ in data space, and loss against iteration. Parameter convergence,
visual fit, and training history answer different questions and should not be
collapsed into one plot.
\end{workedexample}

\section{Batch, stochastic, and mini-batch gradients}

If
\[
J(\theta)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\ell_i(\theta),
\]
batch gradient descent evaluates every $\nabla\ell_i$ before one update. A
stochastic update samples index $I_t$ and uses
\[
g_t=\nabla\ell_{I_t}(\theta_t).
\]
Under uniform sampling,
$\mathbb{E}[g_t\mid\theta_t]=\nabla J(\theta_t)$: the stochastic gradient is
unbiased, though noisy. A mini-batch averages several sampled gradients to
trade variance for parallel efficiency.

\begin{proposition}[Independent mini-batching reduces gradient variance]
Fix $\theta$ and let $G_1,\ldots,G_B$ be independent gradient samples with
mean $\nabla J(\theta)$ and covariance matrix $\Sigma(\theta)$. Their average
$\bar G_B=B^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^BG_j$ is unbiased and has covariance
$\Sigma(\theta)/B$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Linearity of expectation gives
$\mathbb{E}[\bar G_B]=\nabla J(\theta)$. Independence removes cross-covariance
terms, so
\[
\operatorname{Cov}(\bar G_B)
=\frac{1}{B^2}\sum_{j=1}^B\operatorname{Cov}(G_j)
=\frac{1}{B^2}B\Sigma(\theta)=\frac{\Sigma(\theta)}{B}.
\]
Sampling without replacement changes the expression by a finite-population
correction, but the same variance-reduction principle remains.
\end{proof}

One pass through the training observations is an \term{epoch}. An epoch is not
one update unless the batch is the entire dataset. Shuffling affects the
sequence of stochastic gradients, so the seed and data order belong to the
reproducibility record.

Constant-step stochastic methods generally fluctuate near a solution because
gradient noise remains. Decreasing schedules can support convergence under
conditions; modern practice also uses warmup, momentum, adaptive scaling, and
averaging. Their convenience does not remove the need to monitor the actual
objective and update magnitude.

\toprule

\midrule
Full batch & Exact gradient of the finite-sample objective & Expensive update;

Stochastic & Cheap updates and immediate online use & High variance and strong

\section{Momentum and acceleration remain first-order methods}

Plain gradient descent discards its preceding displacement. Polyak's
\term{heavy-ball method} carries a velocity:
\[
v_{t+1}=\beta v_t-\eta\nabla J(\theta_t),
\qquad
\theta_{t+1}=\theta_t+v_{t+1},
\]
where $0\le\beta<1$. On a long narrow valley, gradients alternate across the
steep direction while agreeing along the flat direction. The velocity tends to
cancel the alternating component and accumulate the persistent one. This is a
geometric explanation, not a guarantee for every nonconvex objective.

For the scalar quadratic $J(w)=aw^2/2$, eliminating velocity gives the error
recurrence
\[
e_{t+1}=(1+\beta-\eta a)e_t-\beta e_{t-1}.
\]
Its behavior is governed by the roots of
$r^2-(1+\beta-\eta a)r+\beta=0$. Momentum therefore introduces a second state
and a second stability question. A value of $\beta$ that helps one curvature
can destabilize another; momentum is not free speed.

A common look-ahead form evaluates the gradient where velocity is about to
move the parameters:
\[
\widetilde\theta_t=\theta_t+\beta v_t,
\qquad
v_{t+1}=\beta v_t-\eta\nabla J(\widetilde\theta_t),
\qquad
\theta_{t+1}=\theta_t+v_{t+1}.
\]
This is useful for teaching the intuition behind Nesterov-style momentum, but
the original accelerated convex method uses a particular sequence of
coefficients and proof invariant. Similar-looking library updates should not be
credited with a theorem until their exact recurrence and assumptions match it.

For a convex function with $L$-Lipschitz gradient, fixed-step gradient descent
has a worst-case objective gap of order $1/t$. Nesterov's accelerated method
achieves order $1/t^2$ under the same smooth-convex setting. If the objective is
also $\mu$-strongly convex, appropriately tuned methods converge geometrically,
with the accelerated dependence governed by $\sqrt{L/\mu}$ rather than
$L/\mu$. These are worst-case upper bounds under mathematical assumptions, not
promises about elapsed time. Extra state, more complex kernels, stochastic
noise, and hardware utilization can change the engineering result.

\section{Back-propagation differentiates; the optimizer updates}

For a composition
\[
x=h_0\longmapsto h_1=f_1(h_0;\theta_1)
\longmapsto\cdots\longmapsto
h_L=f_L(h_{L-1};\theta_L)\longmapsto J,
\]
the forward pass computes and stores intermediate values. Reverse-mode
automatic differentiation starts from the scalar derivative
$\partial J/\partial J=1$ and repeatedly applies the chain rule. If
$\bar h_k=\partial J/\partial h_k$, then
\[
\bar h_{k-1}
=\left(\frac{\partial h_k}{\partial h_{k-1}}\right)^T\bar h_k,
\qquad
\nabla_{\theta_k}J
=\left(\frac{\partial h_k}{\partial\theta_k}\right)^T\bar h_k.
\]
This backward sweep computes gradients for every parameter while reusing local
derivatives. Back-propagation is therefore a differentiation procedure, not a
particular optimizer. Gradient descent, mini-batch SGD, momentum, or Adam may
consume the resulting gradient. Rumelhart, Hinton, and Williams' 1986 account
made this reverse error-propagation view influential for learning internal
representations in multilayer networks.
\footnote{D. E. Rumelhart, G. E. Hinton, and R. J. Williams, ``Learning
representations by back-propagating errors,'' \emph{Nature}, 323, 1986,
\url{https://doi.org/10.1038/323533a0}.}

The one-neuron derivation is the smallest complete example of this process:
the loss sends an error derivative to the identity activation, then to the
affine weights and bias. Hidden layers add compositions, not a new chain rule.
This distinction is an engineering boundary too: gradient tests validate the
differentiation component, while optimizer tests validate state updates,
schedules, and termination behavior.

\section{Applications beyond fitting a line}

Gradient methods appear whenever a useful objective is differentiable or has a
tractable derivative surrogate:

\begin{itemize}
  \item logistic regression minimizes a convex likelihood-based loss;
  \item matrix factorization estimates latent structure in recommendation and
  imaging;
  \item inverse problems adjust hidden physical parameters so a simulator
  matches measurements;
  \item optimal control and differentiable simulation optimize trajectories or
  designs;
  \item neural networks compose many parameterized maps and obtain derivatives
  by reverse-mode automatic differentiation; and
  \item variational scientific models optimize an energy or evidence bound.
\end{itemize}

Each application changes the meaning of the objective and the consequences of
local minima, constraints, and stochasticity. The update formula alone does not
validate the model.

\section{Stopping and diagnosing responsibly}

Useful diagnostics include training and validation objectives, gradient norm,
step norm, parameter norm, nonfinite values, and elapsed resources. A stopping
rule might require a small relative objective change and small gradient norm,
with a maximum iteration budget. Validation-based early stopping is also model
selection and must remain inside the development boundary.

\begin{warningbox}
Optimizer convergence establishes only that the implemented search reached a
stationary or sufficiently stable point for its objective. It does not show
that gradients are correct, the objective is scientifically appropriate, the
data are representative, or predictions generalize.
\end{warningbox}

For a hand-built optimizer, verify derivatives three ways when practical:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item derive them symbolically with shapes shown;
  \item compare against centered finite differences on a tiny problem; and
  \item compare the final fitted result with a trusted solver on a convex case.
\end{enumerate}

Finite differences are diagnostic, not the preferred large-scale training
method. Their truncation and floating-point errors depend on perturbation size.

\begin{professionalbox}
A reusable optimizer should report termination reason, iterations, final
objective, gradient or update norm, and nonfinite failure. Record the objective,
data version, preprocessing, initialization, seed, batch construction,
learning-rate schedule, precision, and software version. Silently returning the
last iterate after divergence is an interface failure.
\end{professionalbox}

\section{Measure optimizer performance without moving the goalposts}

An optimizer benchmark needs both a quality axis and a work axis. Objective
against iteration can be misleading because one full-batch iteration and one
single-example update use very different work. Useful horizontal axes include
examples processed, gradient evaluations, matrix-vector products, floating-
point operations, and wall-clock time. Report at least one implementation-
independent work measure alongside elapsed time.

The primary engineering quantity is often \term{time to quality}: elapsed time
until a declared objective, validation metric, or gradient-norm threshold is
first met. Also measure examples per second, peak accelerator or host memory,
data-loader wait, and the fraction of time spent in forward, backward, and
update stages. A profiler should identify the bottleneck before batch size,
precision, compilation, or distribution strategy is changed.

Comparisons must hold the mathematical problem fixed. Use the same training
rows, preprocessing, initialization policy, objective normalization, stopping
target, and numerical precision unless the experiment explicitly studies one
of those choices. Repeat stochastic runs and show their distribution. Count
failed or nonfinite runs rather than deleting them. Separate training
performance from generalization: reaching lower training loss faster is an
optimization result, while improved held-out error is a statistical result.

\section{Exercises}

Use the \textbf{GD} identifier to connect a mathematical result or software
extension to its review history. Contribution problems are deliberately open
enough for original design but narrow enough for a first professional PR.

\subsection*{Intuition and explanation}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{GD-I\arabic*.},leftmargin=*,itemsep=0.65em]
  \item Explain why the gradient is not an arrow to the global minimum. Use a
  convex quadratic, a saddle, and a rescaled coordinate system in the answer.
  \item Distinguish the model, objective, differentiation procedure, and
  optimizer for the identity neuron. Which object changes when plain gradient
  descent is replaced by momentum?
  \item An optimizer reaches gradient norm $10^{-8}$ while held-out RMSE is
  poor. Give four compatible explanations at different layers of the learning
  system.
  \item Compare an epoch, update, example processed, gradient evaluation, and
  second of wall time. Explain why none is universally the fairest horizontal
  axis for an optimizer plot.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Mathematical reasoning}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{GD-M\arabic*.},leftmargin=*,itemsep=0.65em]
  \item For $J(w)=a(w-w^*)^2/2$, derive Theorem~\ref{thm:scalar-gd} and classify
  monotone convergence, oscillatory convergence, one-step convergence,
  stagnation, and divergence in terms of $1-\eta a$.
  \item Generalize Theorem~\ref{thm:scalar-gd} to a positive-definite quadratic
  by diagonalizing its Hessian. Derive the stable interval and the optimal
  constant step in terms of extreme eigenvalues.
  \item Prove Theorem~\ref{thm:steepest-descent} for a weighted norm
  $\lVert d\rVert_M=(d^TMd)^{1/2}$. Identify the corresponding preconditioned
  direction and explain the role of units.
  \item Derive the weight and bias gradients for a mini-batch identity neuron,
  annotating every array shape. Then derive the reverse-mode chain rule if the
  identity activation is replaced by a differentiable scalar $\phi$.
  \item Derive the heavy-ball characteristic polynomial for a scalar quadratic.
  Use its roots to identify one stable and one unstable pair $(\eta,\beta)$.
  Explain why momentum adds state without using a Hessian.
  \item Extend the mini-batch variance proposition to unequal covariance
  matrices. Then state how sampling without replacement alters the independent
  calculation.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Data and engineering investigations}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{GD-C\arabic*.},leftmargin=*,itemsep=0.65em]
  \item Reproduce Figure~\ref{fig:gradient-methods}. Add one deliberately
  unstable configuration and explain the failure from eigenvalues or recurrence
  roots before running it.
  \item Train the single identity neuron on the real diabetes table and compare
  its predictions with a stable least-squares solver. Verify analytic gradients
  by centered finite differences on a tiny subset and justify the tolerance.
  \item Compare batch, stochastic, and mini-batch methods at equal examples
  processed and equal wall time. Repeat stochastic runs, report failures, and
  separate time to training quality from held-out performance.
  \item Profile a training step into data loading, forward pass, backward pass,
  and update. Change one of batch size, numerical precision, or data layout only
  after identifying the bottleneck; report the before-and-after evidence.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Contribution studio}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{GD-PR\arabic*.},leftmargin=*,itemsep=0.65em]
  \item Add a first-order optimizer component with a typed configuration and
  result record containing termination reason, iterations, objective history,
  gradient or update norm, elapsed time, and nonfinite failure. Test a convex
  quadratic, an invalid shape, and divergence.
  \item Add one optimizer extension--a schedule, Polyak momentum, gradient
  clipping, averaging, or a justified alternative. The issue must state the
  recurrence and source; differential tests must compare it with a transparent
  reference implementation.
  \item Add a benchmark recorder that compares optimizers without conflating
  iterations, examples, gradient evaluations, and time. Its output must retain
  configuration, seed, software version, failures, and raw repeated measurements.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{chapterreview}
Gradient descent follows the negative gradient because it minimizes the local
linear approximation under Euclidean geometry. Curvature determines whether a
finite step decreases the objective and how rapidly different directions
converge. Least squares exposes this behavior exactly through $X^TX/n$ and
connects an identity-activation neuron to a familiar estimator. Stochastic and
mini-batch methods estimate the full gradient more cheaply, but introduce
variance and additional reproducibility choices. Momentum carries state and
can accelerate favorable geometry without becoming a second-order method.
Back-propagation computes derivatives; an optimizer decides how to use them.
Optimization success and time to training quality remain distinct from
statistical generalization and scientific validity.
\end{chapterreview}
